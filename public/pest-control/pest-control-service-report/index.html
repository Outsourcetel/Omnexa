About this guide

What this guide covers

A service report is your evidence that the work was done, what was used and what to do next. For homes it is peace of mind; for restaurants, hotels and warehouses it is part of the file an inspector or auditor reads. Here is what a good one contains.

Reading it

How to read a report

Findings

Were pests found, how many, and where? Compare with the previous visit.

Actions

What was done in response, and did it address the cause or only the symptom?

Recommendations

What is your part? Items for you or your landlord are listed here.

Follow-up

When will the next check happen, and what will it confirm?

Products and safety

Product information and safety

A good report names every product used, with its registration number and the areas where it was applied. Public health pesticides carry an SFDA registration number on the label, so you can check it matches.

The report should also give the re-entry time, any ventilation needed and instructions for children, pets and food areas. If something is not clear, ask the technician before they leave.

Commercial sites

Extra records for restaurants, hotels and warehouses

Food-safety and hospitality audits look for more than a one-page report. The file should also hold:

  • A site plan with numbered monitoring points
  • Monitoring results for each point, visit by visit
  • Trend analysis of activity
  • A log of sightings reported by your staff
  • Corrective actions with dates and who closed them
  • The contract, scope of service, and the provider's qualification

Warning signs

Red flags in a poor report

Ask for a better report if you see any of these:

  • No date, no signature or no technician name
  • Products listed without registration numbers, or not listed at all
  • No mention of which areas were treated
  • Only a generic statement such as 'service done'
  • No recommendations, even though conditions need attention
  • No follow-up date after a treatment that needs one

Next steps

What to do with the report

A report is only useful if you act on it:

  • Read the recommendations and fix what is yours to fix
  • File it with previous reports, in date order
  • Share relevant points with staff, such as sighting reporting
  • Compare each report with the last to see the trend
  • Keep it ready for inspections, audits and insurers
  • Call us if the report and what you see do not match

Check your provider

A report is only as reliable as the company behind it. Ask any provider to show you its Balady qualification, and check that public health pesticides carry an SFDA registration number on the label.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Should I get a report after every visit?

Yes. A dated report after each visit shows what was found, what was done and what remains. We provide one after every visit.

Why do the products and registration numbers matter?

They let you check what was applied and where, and show that the products are registered. Public health pesticides carry an SFDA registration number on the label.

Is a paper or digital report better?

Either works if it is complete, dated and signed. Digital copies are easier to store and share with auditors.

How long should I keep reports?

Keep them for as long as your auditors, insurers or customers require. For food and hospitality sites, keep them together in one pest control file.

What if a report says nothing was found?

A report should still record what was inspected and where, so you know the check was done. A clean finding is also useful evidence.
